How to Use This

The clinical content is downstream of the method. Read this section once, then let the briefs reinforce it.

The three habits

  1. The assassin scan. For each answer choice, find the sentence in the stem that makes it impossible. When every option but one has an assassin, the question is finished — no forward reasoning required. A stem's "normal" findings are eliminations, not filler.
  2. The switch rule. Change an answer only when rereading reveals a misread fact — never when it reveals a new possibility. A detail that is merely compatible with another diagnosis is flavor. Incompatibility, not plausibility.
  3. Store the discriminator, not the outcome. Learn "limping child → score Kocher," never "limping child → aspirate." Rules stored as outcomes fire on the benign mirror-twin stem and get you twice.

Read the verb before the choices

  1. "Most likely diagnosis" → the diagnosis that explains all findings, not the most findings.
  2. "Most sensitive / screening" → lowest detection threshold. "Most accurate / definitive / confirms" → tissue, or the gold-standard test.
  3. "Next step in management" → the next rung on the ladder. "Next step in diagnosis" → the test whose result changes what you do tonight.
  4. "Most beneficial / reduces mortality" → match the intervention's time-to-benefit to the outcome's time horizon. Lipids = years. BP, volume, GDMT = weeks to months.
  5. "Underlying cause" → the trunk of the causal tree, not a branch.

Polymyalgia Rheumatica

Elderly · proximal stiffness · ESR

The decision point

Age >50 + bilateral proximal girdle aching with morning stiffness + systemic symptoms + ESR ≥50, with no true weakness and no muscle tenderness. The trap is confusing "can't get out of bed" (pain and stiffness) with weakness (myositis). Treatment is low-dose prednisone ~15 mg; a dramatic response within days is nearly diagnostic.

Shoulder Pain: the Range-of-Motion Rule

Aquifer FM 25 · rotator cuff · impingement

The decision point

One rule sorts every shoulder stem: active ROM lost with passive preserved = muscle/tendon (rotator cuff tendinopathy or tear, biceps tendinitis, impingement). Both active and passive lost = joint (adhesive capsulitis, glenohumeral OA, septic joint). Then the maneuvers map one-to-one onto muscles.

Management ladder & the sling trap First-line is relative rest + physical therapy — strengthen the cuff and scapular stabilizers to open the subacromial space. Transferable rule The exam answer is usually the boring next rung: activity modification beats PT beats injection beats MRI beats surgery.

Biceps Tendinitis

Overuse tendinopathy · initial management

The decision point

Anterior shoulder pain + bicipital groove tenderness + pain on resisted elbow flexion/supination in someone still doing the offending activity. "Initial step" questions reward the least-intensive appropriate intervention: activity modification (stop the throwing and lifting) → NSAIDs → PT if not improving in 2–4 weeks → injection → imaging.

Scenario bank
  1. 26 yo M pitcher, anterior shoulder pain, bicipital groove tenderness, still throwing weekly → activity modification plus NSAIDs
  2. 26 yo M, still painful after 6 weeks of activity modification and NSAIDs → physical therapy
  3. 62 yo M, sudden pop while lifting, bulge in the mid upper arm → long head of biceps rupture → non-operative in older adults
  4. 26 yo M, pain reproduced by resisted supination with the elbow flexed → biceps tendinitis
  5. 45 yo M, anterior shoulder pain with a positive Hawkins test → subacromial impingement
  6. 26 yo M with biceps tendinitis; the question asks the least intensive appropriate step → activity modification
Exam logic vs. clinic reality On the exam, PT is not wrong — it's not initial when the patient is still throwing every weekend. In clinic, modern tendinopathy management is relative rest plus progressive loading, and the two aren't really opposed. What matters at the bedside: say the words "do not wear a sling." Patients hear "rest" and immobilize, and the shoulder is uniquely punished for immobilization — adhesive capsulitis can set in within weeks.
Transferable rule Give patients a positive instruction (what to do), not just a negative one (what to avoid), or they will invent their own version of rest.

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

Median neuropathy at the wrist

The decision point

Read the verb. "Confirm the diagnosis" → nerve conduction studies. "Next step in management" → nocturnal neutral wrist splint. "Next step" with thenar atrophy → surgical release. Phalen and Tinel are ~50–60% sensitive, so negative maneuvers cannot overturn a classic history.

The anatomy fact they build questions on CTS spares sensation over the thenar eminence, because the palmar cutaneous branch arises proximal to the tunnel and runs over the retinaculum. Numbness that includes the thenar eminence means a proximal lesion — pronator teres syndrome. Motor loss is late: LOAF (Lumbricals 1–2, Opponens, Abductor pollicis brevis, Flexor pollicis brevis).

De Quervain Tendinopathy

First dorsal compartment · APL + EPB

The decision point

Radial styloid tenderness + positive Finkelstein/Eichhoff (thumb flexed, wrist ulnar-deviated) is a clinical diagnosis. No imaging, no labs. Treatment: thumb spica splint + NSAIDs → corticosteroid injection → first dorsal compartment release.

The restraint answer "No additional testing," "reassurance," "proceed with the procedure," and "continue current management" are a family of correct answers. They win when the diagnosis is clinical, a provocative test is positive, and there are no red flags. The instinct to "get an X-ray to be safe" adds only incidental findings.

Meralgia Paresthetica

Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve · L2–L3

The decision point

The LFCN is purely sensory — compressed under the inguinal ligament near the ASIS. No motor fibers means no weakness is possible, so 5/5 strength is confirmatory, not reassuring incidental data. Clinical diagnosis; conservative management resolves >90%.

Scaphoid Fracture

FOOSH · the negative film

The decision point

FOOSH + anatomic snuffbox tenderness = scaphoid fracture until proven otherwise, and a negative initial X-ray proves nothing — up to 20–25% are radiographically occult in week one. Answer: thumb spica cast anyway, re-image in 2–3 weeks (or MRI now, if offered).

Scenario bank
  1. 22 yo M after a FOOSH, snuffbox tenderness, X-ray negative → thumb spica cast and re-image in 2–3 weeks
  2. 22 yo M, same presentation, with MRI among the options → MRI now
  3. 22 yo M with a missed scaphoid fracture presenting 6 months later with wrist pain → avascular necrosis and nonunion
  4. 22 yo M after a FOOSH with distal radius tenderness and dorsal angulation → Colles fracture
  5. Which scaphoid fracture site carries the highest avascular necrosis risk → proximal pole
  6. 22 yo M with a displaced scaphoid waist fracture on X-ray → surgical fixation
Why the stakes justify casting on suspicion The scaphoid's blood supply enters distally and runs retrograde to the proximal pole. A missed fracture means avascular necrosis and nonunion. Highest AVN risk = proximal pole. The AVN club worth memorizing: femoral head, scaphoid proximal pole, talus, humeral head — all retrograde or tenuous supply.
Transferable rule The D-dimer principle in orthopedic clothing: a negative test cannot rule out disease when pretest probability is high. High-probability PE → CTA regardless of dimer; high-probability scaphoid → cast regardless of film.

Low Back Pain: Red Flags

When to image · when not to

The decision point

Acute low back pain (<4 weeks) gets no imaging — unless a red flag fires. Red flags for malignancy do not require a neurologic deficit; early vertebral metastasis is neurologically silent. A normal exam only rules out when its sensitivity exceeds your pretest probability, and that probability is set by the past medical history.

Hip Positioning: Fracture vs. Dislocation

Free points, frequently reversed

The decision point

Shortened + externally rotated + abducted = hip fracture (femoral neck / intertrochanteric). Shortened + internally rotated + adducted + flexed = posterior hip dislocation — the dashboard injury, ~90% of hip dislocations, with the sciatic nerve at risk.

Mnemonic and the shoulder mirror Fracture = Falls outward (external). Posterior dislocation = Points inward (internal). The shoulder mirrors it: anterior dislocation (95%) = abducted + externally rotated; posterior dislocation (seizures, electrocution) = adducted + internally rotated. In both joints the common and rare forms are mirror images.
Scenario bank
  1. 78 yo F after a fall, leg shortened and externally rotated → femoral neck fracture → X-ray, then arthroplasty
  2. 28 yo M after a head-on collision, leg shortened, internally rotated, adducted and flexed → posterior hip dislocation → reduction within 6 hours
  3. 28 yo M with foot drop after a posterior hip dislocation → sciatic nerve injury
  4. 78 yo F with a displaced femoral neck fracture → arthroplasty → retinacular vessel disruption risks AVN
  5. 28 yo M whose hip dislocation was reduced at 10 hours → increased risk of femoral head osteonecrosis
  6. 70 yo M after a fall, leg shortened and externally rotated but abducted, groin pain → intertrochanteric fracture
Time-critical Posterior dislocation needs reduction within ~6 hours to avoid femoral head AVN. Femoral neck fracture in the elderly → AVN risk from retinacular vessel disruption → arthroplasty.

Greater Trochanteric Pain Syndrome

Findings beat pain descriptors

The decision point

Point tenderness over the greater trochanter with a normal neurologic exam. Stems plant "burning," which sounds neuropathic and pulls toward meralgia paresthetica — but burning is a quality descriptor, while tenderness and a normal neuro exam are findings. GTPS is a tendinopathy; meralgia is a neuropathy.

The location rule that organizes all hip pain: groin → the joint itself (OA, AVN, fracture, labral tear) · lateral → trochanter and its tendons, or the LFCN · posterior/buttock → spine, SI joint, or piriformis.

Inflammatory Back Pain & Spondyloarthritis

Two causes of night pain, opposite behavior

The decision point

Inflammatory back pain is defined by its relationship to rest and activity: age <40, insidious onset >3 months, worse at night and with rest, better with movement — the exact inverse of mechanical pain. The mechanism is enthesitis: inflammation where tendons and ligaments insert into bone, as opposed to RA's synovitis.

The discriminator worth extracting Both inflammatory back pain and malignancy wake patients at night. The separator: inflammatory pain improves once you get up and move; malignant pain is unrelieved, and comes with age >50, prior cancer, or weight loss. Same symptom, opposite response to activity.
The spondyloarthritis family — "PAIR" Psoriatic arthritis · Ankylosing spondylitis · IBD-associated · Reactive arthritis. All seronegative (RF-negative), HLA-B27-associated, sharing enthesitis, dactylitis, and uveitis.
Ankylosing spondylitis
  • Young men; HLA-B27 ~90% (supportive, not diagnostic)
  • Sacroiliitis is the earliest finding → X-ray of the SI joints first; MRI if the X-ray is normal and suspicion is high
  • Exam: reduced Schober test, reduced chest expansion, loss of lumbar lordosis; late bamboo spine
  • Extra-articular: anterior uveitis (25–40%), aortitis → aortic regurgitation, AV block, apical pulmonary fibrosis, IgA nephropathy, and a rigid spine that fractures with minor trauma
  • Treatment: NSAIDs + exercise first-line → TNF-α or IL-17 inhibitors. Methotrexate and sulfasalazine work for peripheral disease but not axial. Screen for latent TB before a TNF inhibitor.

Osteoarthritis Pharmacotherapy

Class vs. member

The decision point

A keyed answer can look like it contradicts a guideline when the right class is represented by the wrong member. "NSAIDs are first-line for knee OA" is correct — but if the only NSAID offered is ketorolac, a drug with a hard 5-day maximum because of GI, renal, and bleeding risk, then it is disqualified for a 6-month condition in a 72-year-old, and acetaminophen wins by default.

Guideline currency ACR/Arthritis Foundation 2019 strongly recommends topical NSAIDs for knee OA (upgraded from conditional in 2012) and strongly recommends oral NSAIDs, while acetaminophen was downgraded to conditional — framed for patients unable to take NSAIDs, with small effect sizes in trials. Older NBME items still key acetaminophen. Patient factors here also favor it: age 72 (GI, renal, CV risk) and pain rated 3/10.

The Acute Hot Joint (adult)

Aspirate before you treat

The decision point

An acute hot, swollen, erythematous joint with severe pain on passive range of motion and fever gets aspirated — and the reason is stronger than "this looks septic." Nothing on exam can exclude infection, gout and septic arthritis overlap completely, and a missed septic joint destroys cartilage in days. Aspiration is diagnostic and therapeutic.

The comorbidities point the opposite way from how they read. Obesity, hypertension, and diabetes feel like "metabolic → gout" — but diabetes is a septic arthritis risk factor, and joint space narrowing on x-ray means preexisting joint disease, another one.

Septic Arthritis of the Hip

Surgical emergency · Kocher criteria

The decision point

Score the Kocher criteria: fever >38.5 °C, non-weight-bearing, ESR >40, WBC >12,000 (CRP >2.0 often added as a fifth). Three criteria ≈ 93% probability, four ≈ 99% → arthrocentesis. Hip held flexed, abducted, externally rotated maximizes capsular volume — but that position occurs in transient synovitis too and discriminates nothing.

Scenario bank
  1. 3 yo M, T 39.1, refuses to bear weight, ESR 62, WBC 15,000 → Kocher 4 of 4 → arthrocentesis before antibiotics
  2. 3 yo M, febrile, hip held flexed, abducted and externally rotated → septic arthritis of the hip → urgent aspiration
  3. 3 yo M with a febrile hip; ultrasound shows an effusion → an effusion does not distinguish the twins → score Kocher and aspirate
  4. 3 yo M with culture-confirmed septic arthritis of the hip → surgical drainage plus IV antibiotics
  5. 2 yo M with sickle cell disease, fever and hip pain → add Salmonella coverage
  6. 10 mo F, afebrile, asymmetric thigh folds, positive Ortolani → developmental dysplasia of the hip → ultrasound
  7. 3 yo M with a septic hip; why delay is dangerous → femoral head osteonecrosis from raised intracapsular pressure
Sequencing Aspirate before antibiotics. Synovial fluid >50,000 WBC (PMN-predominant) confirms it. Septic hip is a surgical emergency — delayed drainage causes femoral head AVN and growth arrest. Organisms: S. aureus overall, Kingella <4 years, gonococcus in adolescents, Salmonella in sickle cell disease.
Septic joint vs. osteomyelitis Septic joint = passive ROM excruciating. Osteomyelitis = ROM relatively preserved with point tenderness over the metaphysis.

Transient Synovitis Mirror twin

The benign version of the same stem

The decision point

Kocher 0–1 of 4. Well-appearing, afebrile, limps but walks, post-viral URI 1–2 weeks prior. A small effusion on ultrasound is a decoy — it is present in both twins. Management is NSAIDs and follow-up.

Nursemaid's Elbow

Radial head subluxation · reading the exam block

The decision point

In the newer "Patient Information" format, the treatment is often buried inside the physical exam section. "When the forearm is supinated and the elbow flexed… she begins to reach for toys 15 minutes later" is the reduction and is the confirmation it worked. The answer is what comes after a successful reduction: nothing.

  • Mechanism: axial traction on an extended, pronated arm; peak ages 1–4, rare after 5
  • Pathology: annular ligament slips over the radial head into the radiocapitellar joint
  • Exam: arm held extended and pronated, with no swelling, no deformity, no point tenderness — those three absences permit a clinical diagnosis
  • Reduction: hyperpronation, or supination + flexion; use returns within minutes
  • After success: no imaging, no sling, no NSAIDs, no restrictions. Recurrence ~25%
  • If reduction fails (two attempts) → X-ray for occult fracture
Scenario bank
  1. 3 yo F pulled up by the wrist, holds the arm pronated, refuses to use it, no swelling → radial head subluxation → supination-flexion reduction
  2. 3 yo F reaching for toys 15 minutes after the manoeuvre → the reduction was the treatment → no imaging needed
  3. 3 yo F with elbow swelling and refusal to move it after a fall onto an outstretched hand → supracondylar fracture → elbow X-ray
  4. 3 yo F with a supracondylar fracture and an absent radial pulse → brachial artery injury → urgent orthopedic evaluation
  5. 3 yo F with a second episode of nursemaid's elbow this year → counsel against axial traction — recurrence is common
  6. 10 mo M with a spiral humerus fracture and an inconsistent history → non-accidental trauma → skeletal survey
When it is NOT nursemaid's Swelling, ecchymosis, or deformity · point tenderness over bone · age >5 · a mechanism that doesn't fit the injury, or no mechanism at all → consider non-accidental trauma. Any fracture in a non-ambulatory infant → skeletal survey (<2 yrs) and report. Abuse patterns: metaphyseal corner / bucket-handle, posterior rib fractures, multiple fractures at different healing stages.
Pediatric elbow neighbors Supracondylar humerus fracture — 5–8 yrs, FOOSH hyperextension, posterior fat pad sign; check the anterior interosseous nerve ("OK sign") and brachial artery; watch for Volkmann ischemic contracture · newborn not moving an arm after shoulder dystocia with an asymmetric Moro → clavicle fracture or brachial plexus injury (Erb C5–C6 "waiter's tip"; Klumpke C8–T1 claw hand).

Kawasaki Disease

Medium-vessel vasculitis · a 10-day clock

The decision point

Fever ≥5 days + ≥4 of 5 criteria. IVIG must be given within 10 days of fever onset — this is an act-now answer, not a work-up answer. Sterile pyuria is a feature, not a distractor: KD causes urethritis, giving urinary WBCs with no bacteria.

CRASH and Burn
  • Conjunctivitis — bilateral, nonexudative, limbal sparing
  • Rash — polymorphous, perineal accentuation with desquamation
  • Adenopathy — cervical ≥1.5 cm, usually unilateral (the least common criterion)
  • Strawberry tongue / cracked red lips — no ulcers, no exudate
  • Hands and feet — erythema and edema acutely, periungual desquamation in week 2–3
  • Burn — fever ≥5 days

  5. 4 yo M with fever, conjunctivitis, Koplik spots and a descending rash → measles

  7. 4 yo M with fever, shock, rash and multiorgan involvement weeks after COVID-19 → MIS-C

Pediatric Upper Respiratory Infection

Green mucus is not a bacterial sign

The decision point

Purulent or colored discharge does not mean bacterial. Green comes from myeloperoxidase in neutrophils recruited by any inflammation, viral included. Afebrile + well-appearing + 2 days + clear mobile TMs + no exudate + clear lungs → viral URI → supportive care.

When a "cold" earns antibiotics — three doors
  • Acute bacterial sinusitis: symptoms ≥10 days without improvement, OR high fever + purulence ≥3–4 consecutive days, OR "double worsening." Clinical diagnosis — no imaging.
  • Strep pharyngitis: Centor (fever, exudate, tender anterior nodes, no cough) → test before treating. Treatment prevents rheumatic fever, not PSGN.
  • Acute otitis media: bulging, immobile, erythematous TM. Mobility on pneumatic otoscopy is the discriminator.
Scenario bank
  1. 3 yo M, 2 days of green nasal discharge, afebrile, well, clear mobile tympanic membranes → viral upper respiratory infection → supportive care
  2. 3 yo M with 12 days of nasal discharge and no improvement → acute bacterial rhinosinusitis → amoxicillin-clavulanate
  3. 3 yo M with a bulging, immobile, erythematous tympanic membrane and fever → acute otitis media
  4. 3 yo M who improved then developed new fever and worsening discharge on day 7 → double worsening — bacterial sinusitis
  5. 3 yo M with clear rhinorrhea, sneezing, itchy eyes and seasonal recurrence → allergic rhinitis → intranasal corticosteroid
  6. A parent asks whether green discharge proves bacterial infection → no — colour reflects neutrophil myeloperoxidase in any inflammation

Asthma: Severity Classification

Exam answer vs. current guideline

The decision point

Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis

The stereotype that stems are built to defeat

The decision point

"SCFE = obese teenager" is a stereotype stored as a rule, and question-writers defeat it deliberately. The physis is mechanically weak during any rapid growth spurt, so tall, thin adolescents get SCFE too. The real discriminators are age at the growth spurt (girls 11–13, boys 13–15), loss of internal rotation, and obligate external rotation on passive flexion — not body habitus.

Two familiar decoys "Runny nose last week" — the post-URI transient synovitis bait, planted where the age and a 3-week course forbid it. "Fell while skateboarding" — the fracture bait; minor trauma exacerbating a weeks-long insidious problem is classic SCFE, whereas a pediatric femoral neck fracture needs high-impact trauma and shows a fracture line.
  • Pathology: the epiphysis displaces posteroinferiorly through the hypertrophic zone of the physis — "ice cream slipping off the cone"
  • Imaging: AP + frog-leg lateral of BOTH hips. Klein's line along the superior femoral neck should intersect the epiphysis — it fails to in SCFE
  • Stable vs. unstable: can bear weight vs. cannot bear weight — unstable carries AVN risk approaching 50%
  • Treatment: immediate non-weight-bearing (do not let the patient walk) plus urgent in situ screw fixation. Never attempt reduction — it increases AVN
  • Bilateral in 20–40% — image the other hip; some pin it prophylactically
  • Complications: AVN, chondrolysis, early osteoarthritis

  3. Contralateral hip asymptomatic → still image it; 20–40% bilateral

  5. Boy 5–7, insidious limp, normal labs, femoral head fragmentation → Legg-Calvé-Perthes

Anginal Equivalents & Stress Test Selection

The trigger localizes better than the location

The decision point

Score the three angina criteria and ignore your instinct about location: (1) provoked by exertion, (2) relieved by rest, (3) typical substernal location. 3/3 = typical, 2/3 = atypical, <2 = nonanginal. "Epigastric burning" is a textbook anginal equivalent — and not relieved by antacids kills the GI story outright.

Scenario bank

  4. 58 yo M with severe asthma requiring pharmacologic stress testing → dobutamine → avoid adenosine and regadenoson

Infective Endocarditis Prophylaxis

Two gates, both must open

The decision point

Prophylaxis requires a high-risk cardiac condition AND a high-risk procedure. MVP with regurgitation, bicuspid valve, rheumatic valve disease, and HCM all came off the list in 2007 — and NBME has been recycling "MVP + dental visit → nothing" ever since.

Gate 1 — high-risk cardiac conditions (the whole list)
  • Prosthetic valve or prosthetic material used in valve repair
  • Prior infective endocarditis
  • Unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease; repaired with residual defect near prosthetic material; or fully repaired with prosthetic material for the first 6 months
  • Cardiac transplant with valvulopathy
Gate 2 — high-risk procedures
  • Dental work with gingival manipulation or mucosal perforation
  • Respiratory procedures breaching mucosa; procedures on infected skin or tissue
  • Not routine GI/GU procedures — that indication died in 2007 too
Scenario bank
  1. 45 yo F with mitral valve prolapse and regurgitation, due for a dental cleaning → no prophylaxis
  2. 45 yo M with a prosthetic aortic valve, due for a dental extraction → amoxicillin 2 g one hour before
  3. 45 yo M with prior infective endocarditis and penicillin allergy, dental cleaning → cephalexin, azithromycin or doxycycline

COPD: Which Interventions Improve Survival

Mortality vs. symptoms

The decision point

"Maximum impact on survival" asks you to rank by outcome, not to treat the disease. Only a handful of things reduce mortality in COPD — and stems often preemptively remove smoking cessation (patient already quit) to force you into the second tier.

Reduce mortality
  • Smoking cessation
  • Long-term oxygen therapy — if criteria met
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation
  • Vaccination (influenza, COVID; pneumococcal)
  • Lung volume reduction surgery / transplant in selected patients
  • Nocturnal NIV for chronic daytime hypercapnia

Everything else — LAMA/LABA, ICS, macrolides, roflumilast, chest physiotherapy — improves symptoms and/or exacerbations with no demonstrated survival benefit.

And normal WBC and platelets make this secondary polycythemia — polycythemia vera would elevate all three lines with low EPO and JAK2 V617F.

Asthma vs. COPD

Objective measurement beats subjective report

The decision point

Asthma and COPD are defined apart by reversibility of airflow obstruction — and reversibility is a measurement, not an impression. That is why spirometry before and after a bronchodilator beats "symptomatic response to a trial of albuterol," even though both involve the same drug: subjective relief does not correlate with the spirometric bronchodilator response.

Notice these stems are deliberately unresolvable clinically — asthma features (seasonal, episodic, eczema, family history) stacked against COPD features (age, pack-years). When the history cannot settle it, the answer must be a test that measures the defining variable, not a proxy marker.

Formerly NAFLD · a diagnosis of exclusion

The decision point

  • Most common chronic liver disease in the US; progression is steatosis → MASH → fibrosis → cirrhosis → HCC, and HCC can occur without cirrhosis
  • Exclude first: alcohol, hepatitis B/C, hemochromatosis, Wilson disease, autoimmune hepatitis, α1-antitrypsin deficiency, and drugs (steroids, amiodarone, methotrexate, tamoxifen)
  • Stage fibrosis with FIB-4 or transient elastography
  • Treatment: 7–10% weight loss reverses steatohepatitis; bariatric surgery if BMI ≥35; GLP-1 agonists; resmetirom for MASH with fibrosis

Reading a three-act chronology

The decision point

TSH lags dose changes by 6–8 weeks. A stem that specifies 8 weeks is telling you the TSH is now trustworthy — so a TSH of 9.5 means the dose is genuinely inadequate → increase it. When a stem conspicuously specifies a timeframe, it's telling you which rule applies, not testing whether you remember that a rule exists.

The real trick set
  • Rechecked too soon (<6 wks after a change) → answer is "recheck in 6 weeks," not a dose change
  • TSH stays high despite escalating doses → adherence, then absorption: empty stomach; iron, calcium, PPIs, cholestyramine bind it; consider celiac disease
  • Pregnancy → requirement rises 30–50% immediately; increase as soon as pregnancy is confirmed
  • Elderly or CAD → start low, go slow (25–50 µg) to avoid angina or arrhythmia
  • Central hypothyroidism → titrate to free T4; TSH is the broken variable
  • Subclinical hypothyroidism (high TSH, normal T4) → treat if TSH >10, TPO+, pregnant, or symptomatic

Hyperprolactinemia

Two-step inference, and a number that discriminates

The decision point

When a stem describes an illness and says the patient "remains on medication" without naming it, the question is a two-step inference: identify the drug class the illness requires, then choose the member whose side-effect profile matches the complaint. Psychosis → antipsychotic → the one with the highest prolactin liability.

The number is the discriminator. Drug-induced hyperprolactinemia runs 25–100 and rarely exceeds 200; a prolactinoma typically produces >200. A level of 70 assassinates the prolactinoma option without any imaging.

Mechanism & the antipsychotic ranking Dopamine is prolactin-inhibiting factor; D2 blockade in the tuberoinfundibular pathway removes that inhibition. Highest prolactin effect: risperidone and paliperidone, plus high-potency typicals like haloperidol. Lowest: aripiprazole (a D2 partial agonist that can lower prolactin), quetiapine, clozapine — which makes aripiprazole the answer to both "least likely to cause this" and "what should you switch to."

Hearing Loss & Otosclerosis

Rinne and Weber before anything else

The decision point

Conductive loss: Weber lateralizes to the affected ear, Rinne shows BC > AC in that ear. Sensorineural: Weber lateralizes away, Rinne stays AC > BC bilaterally. A conductive loss with a normal otoscopic exam in a young adult is otosclerosis.

Otosclerosis pathophysiology A genetic bone-remodeling disorder of the otic capsule at the fissula ante fenestram — an otospongiotic (hypervascular, resorptive) phase followed by a sclerotic phase that fixes the stapes footplate. Autosomal dominant with ~40% penetrance, onset in the 20s–40s, female 2:1 — so family history is often absent. Estrogen and progesterone accelerate it, which is why it is classically unmasked in pregnancy. Measles virus RNA has been found in otosclerotic foci.
Exam & audiogram Paracusis of Willis — hears better in noisy environments · Carhart notch — dip in bone conduction at 2000 Hz, an artifact of ossicular fixation · Schwartze sign — reddish blush from hypervascularity · treatment: amplification or stapedectomy · van der Hoeve syndrome = osteogenesis imperfecta + otosclerosis + blue sclerae (both type I collagen disorders).

The PMH is the precondition

The decision point

Atopic dermatitis + vesicles = eczema herpeticum until proven otherwise. The atopic dermatitis isn't background color — it's the precondition. A disrupted barrier plus local immune dysfunction is what lets HSV spread across skin instead of staying a single cold sore. Treatment is immediate systemic acyclovir, because it can disseminate to liver and brain.

The two assassins "Conjunctiva and oropharynx are clear" kills the drug reaction — SJS/TEN requires mucosal involvement (classically ≥2 sites) plus symmetric target lesions. "Progressive worsening on TMP-SMX" kills impetigo — that drug covers staph including MRSA, and failure on appropriate antibacterial therapy eliminates the bacterial hypothesis, exactly as a failed antihistamine eliminates upper airway cough syndrome.

Ectopic Pregnancy

Location questions need imaging, not numbers

The decision point

Pregnancy is already confirmed by the urine test. The question is no longer "is she pregnant?" but "where is the pregnancy?" — and only one tool answers a location question: transvaginal ultrasound. A single serum β-hCG localizes nothing.

Where β-hCG actually belongs
  1. Symptomatic + positive pregnancy test → TVUS now
  2. Intrauterine pregnancy seen → done (heterotopic is rare outside IVF)
  3. Adnexal mass or free fluid → ectopic, treat
  4. TVUS indeterminate → now β-hCG earns its keep: above the discriminatory zone (~1,500–2,000) with an empty uterus = presumed ectopic; below it → serial hCG q48h (normal IUP doubles; ectopic rises <35–50%; miscarriage falls)
Stability picks the branch Hypotensive, rebound, or shoulder pain (diaphragmatic irritation from hemoperitoneum) → skip everything, emergent laparoscopy/laparotomy. Stable ectopic workup = image; unstable = operate — the same logic as PE.
Pearls Biggest acquired risk factor is prior PID (tubal scarring); also prior ectopic, tubal surgery, IUD in place, smoking (impairs ciliary transport) · half of ectopics have no palpable adnexal mass · methotrexate if unruptured, hCG <5,000, <3.5 cm, no fetal cardiac activity, reliable follow-up · Rh-negative mother with any bleeding in pregnancy → RhoGAM.

Adolescent Abnormal Uterine Bleeding

Reassurance needs a benign cause AND an acceptable consequence

The decision point

Anovulatory cycles from an immature hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis are genuinely normal in the first 1–2 years after menarche — which is why most people choose reassurance. But reassurance requires two things: a benign cause and an acceptable consequence. Anemia is a consequence. The physiology being normal doesn't make the outcome acceptable.

Mechanism: immature axis → no LH surge → anovulation → no corpus luteum → no progesterone → unopposed estrogen drives continuous proliferation with no secretory differentiation → the lining outgrows its blood supply and sheds unpredictably. Progesterone converts proliferative to secretory endometrium and produces organized withdrawal bleeding.

The detail most people skip "Nonpainful." Ovulatory cycles are crampy — painless heavy irregular bleeding is the anovulatory signature. Normal adolescent parameters: cycle length 21–45 days (adults 24–38), duration ≤7 days, ≤3–6 products/day.

Contraception & DMPA

A pregnancy test precedes every contraceptive decision

The decision point

When two explanations produce identical symptoms, test for the one that changes management and can't be missed. DMPA side effects — breast soreness, weight gain, fatigue, amenorrhea — are indistinguishable from early pregnancy. And the arithmetic assassin: DMPA is due every 13 weeks; her last injection was 4 months ago, so she has been uncovered.

The structural rule: a pregnancy test precedes every contraception decision, every amenorrhea workup, and absolutely every IUD placement — inserting an IUD into an established intrauterine pregnancy can disrupt it.

DMPA facts Suppresses GnRH → no LH surge → no ovulation; thickens cervical mucus · every 13 weeks with a ~2-week grace period · irregular bleeding early, then amenorrhea in ~50% at 1 year · weight gain from appetite stimulation · reversible loss of bone mineral density (boxed warning) · the unique one: delayed return to fertility up to 10 months · no estrogen, so safe in smokers ≥35, migraine with aura, VTE history, and immediately postpartum.

Cervical Screening: the Age-21 Floor

An eligibility gate, not a risk calculation

The decision point

A 20-year-old is below the screening floor. Sexual debut at 15, five partners, inconsistent condoms — every one of those raises HPV exposure and none of them lowers the starting age. The answer is no cytology; screening begins at 21.

The rule is deliberately risk-blind. HPV in adolescents is near-universal and clears in ~90% within 2 years; progression to cancer takes a decade-plus. Screening early finds transient infection and produces excisional procedures (LEEP) that raise preterm-birth risk in women who were never going to develop cancer. That's why cervical cancer under 21 is rare — and why screening her would harm without benefit.

Tdap in Pregnancy at 10 Weeks

A window that optimizes, not a gate that excludes

The decision point

She is 10 weeks pregnant and asks about vaccines. Tdap is recommended in every pregnancy, but the antibody transfer that protects the newborn is maximized when it's given at 27–36 weeks, preferably early in that window. So at 10 weeks the answer is administer Tdap in the third trimester — schedule it, don't decline it.

Correction to an earlier framing in this document The Part I pregnancy-vaccine brief listed Tdap's trap as "Timing — 27–36 weeks," which reads like an eligibility gate. It isn't. Tdap is safe at any gestational age, and if a pregnant woman has a wound requiring tetanus prophylaxis, or is caught in a pertussis outbreak, you give it then. 27–36 weeks is an optimization of transplacental IgG transfer, not a permission window. Gate vs. optimization is exactly the distinction NBME builds a question on.

Ranking Preventive Interventions

"Most beneficial" is a magnitude question

The decision point

When every option is guideline-supported, the question is asking you to rank by magnitude of benefit. Nothing beats quitting a pack a day: smoking cessation reduces all-cause mortality more than any drug on the list, cuts CV risk substantially within 1–2 years, and approaches never-smoker risk over ~15 years.

The decision point

Elder Abuse & Mandated Reporting

Capacity determines the path

The decision point

Reasonable suspicion → report to Adult Protective Services. You do not need proof, and you do not investigate first. Confounders like warfarin (easy bruising) and dementia (unreliable historian) are real and irrelevant to the decision — APS investigates; you don't adjudicate.

Scenario bank
  1. 82 yo F with bruises in multiple stages of healing and a vague mechanism → report to Adult Protective Services
  2. 82 yo F on warfarin with easy bruising and an implausible history → warfarin does not remove the obligation to report suspicion
  3. 82 yo F with dementia who denies abuse while her caregiver is present → interview her alone
  4. 34 yo F disclosing intimate partner violence, fully competent, who declines reporting → support and safety planning, respecting her autonomy
  5. 6 yo M with a spiral femur fracture and an inconsistent history → mandatory report to child protective services
  6. 82 yo F with suspected abuse; who investigates → Adult Protective Services, not the physician
The findings that build suspicion Bruises in multiple stages of healing (the single most tested physical finding, as in child abuse) · implausible or vague mechanism · caregiver who cannot explain the injuries · patient who becomes uncomfortable and minimizes when asked alone · risk profile: dementia, dependency, caregiver stress. Interview technique matters and can itself be the answer: separate the patient from the caregiver, ask direct nonjudgmental questions, don't confront the suspected abuser.

Adolescent Confidentiality

Ask what would have to be true to break it

The decision point

A minor discloses substance use during a confidential interview. The answer is keep it confidential and counsel him directly — because substance use is a confidential service in essentially every state, and nothing in the stem triggers an exception. Confidentiality here isn't a courtesy; it's the mechanism that makes honest disclosure possible at all, and adolescents who don't expect it don't disclose.

The right move is procedural, not just permissive: set the frame before he talks ("what you tell me stays between us, unless someone's safety is at risk"), then use the disclosure — screen with CRAFFT, deliver brief counseling, and encourage him to tell his parents himself. Encouraging voluntary disclosure is not the same as disclosing.
