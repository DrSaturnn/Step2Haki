## Q8 Poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis: order C3 (peds-cms-cb45d9)

- **Sort:** covered.
- **Owning brief:** bs-nephritic.
- **Item carrying the decision:** q_aeb588e544ebeb4b9c39. It has an 8-year-old with tea-colored urine, edema and a BP near 138/90, 2 weeks after a sore throat, with red cell casts. The key is serum C3. The key and the deciding finding (a nephritic urine 2 weeks after pharyngitis) are the same. The workup table already lists C3 and C4 as "Next".
- **Write:** link only, with no duplicate question. Add one "How NBME framed it" line, because the decoys differ. The page has no trapline for echocardiography, ANA, urine culture or renal ultrasound.
- **NBME framing lesson:** NBME hides the nephritic picture under AKI chemistry (low bicarbonate, borderline potassium, high BUN) and plants a soft systolic murmur with tachycardia after a strep throat, so echocardiography for rheumatic carditis looks reasonable. The clear lungs are what close that route.
- **Decoys, as the NBME explanation dismisses them:** ANA is neither sensitive nor specific for PSGN. A urine culture is for cystitis or pyelonephritis, and the casts point to the glomerulus. Echocardiography looks for heart failure, which the clear lungs make unlikely. Renal ultrasound shows gross structure (cysts, masses, obstruction), not immune-complex deposits.
- **Conflict:** none. Both the page and the NBME key a low C3. One caution: the NBME explanation says light microscopy shows C3 and IgG deposits, but deposits of named proteins are an immunofluorescence finding. Do not carry that wording onto the page.

## Q9 Kawasaki disease, untreated: why the child is tachypneic (peds-cms-42aa4f)

- **Sort:** new angle.
- **Owning brief:** kawasaki. myocarditis is the related brief for heart failure that presents as respiratory distress.
- **Why this is not covered:** the kawasaki brief covers the acute diagnosis, IVIG within 10 days, the echo schedule and the lower aneurysm risk with treatment. It has no item on the late cardiac course after untreated disease. The myocarditis brief teaches the rule that a gallop reassigns the failing organ, but only for an acute viral illness; there, Kawasaki appears only as a mimic to exclude on its fever gate.
- **What must be taught:**
  - **Deciding finding:** a full Kawasaki illness 2 months earlier that went away without treatment (prolonged high fever, conjunctivitis, red swollen hands, adenopathy, cracked red lips). The child now has tachypnea, tachycardia, hypotension, cardiomegaly and ischemic ST depression.
  - **Key:** congestive heart failure. The fast breathing comes from a failing heart. Untreated Kawasaki can lead to coronary aneurysm expansion, rupture, myocardial infarction and heart failure, which is why suspected disease gets an ECG and echocardiography at once.
  - **Traplines:** pericardial effusion is a real Kawasaki complication, but it gives a tamponade picture (raised JVP, pulsus paradoxus), which is absent here; cardiomegaly with ischemic ST changes fits failure. Mitral valve obstruction: Kawasaki causes mitral regurgitation, not stenosis, and there is no stenotic murmur. Interstitial lung disease and thickened pulmonary arterioles: Kawasaki rarely involves the lungs or the pulmonary vessels.
- **NBME framing lesson:** the lead-in asks for a mechanism, but the key is a syndrome name. The diagnosis sits in a past-tense history marked as resolved on its own, which means it was never treated. The present low-grade fever is a decoy for a new acute illness.
- **Conflict:** none.

## Q10 Calf pain after an influenza-like illness (peds-cms-80d5b8)

- **Sort:** new. No brief owns post-viral myositis. The page has no mention of influenza myositis.
- **Proposed brief:** id bs-flu-myositis, system musculoskeletal, data-bp="msk".
  - **Pairs with:** growing-pains, which is also bilateral calf pain in a school-age child but needs a normal exam. That brief already says tenderness on palpation is incompatible with growing pains.
  - **Also links to:** dipstick-mismatch (rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria), pmr (pain versus weakness, CK), kawasaki, bs-exanthems (parvovirus).
- **What must be taught:**
  - **Deciding finding:** severe bilateral calf pain with marked calf tenderness about a week after a febrile illness with cough, sore throat and headache, in an afebrile child now off medications. This is influenza-induced myositis, which can follow influenza A or B during or after the infection.
  - **Key and confirmation:** influenza-induced myositis. CK is elevated, myoglobinuria may be present, and influenza testing is diagnostic in the right clinical setting. The severity ranges from myalgia with mild weakness to quadriparesis and rhabdomyolysis.
  - **Traplines:**
    - Dermatomyositis gives proximal weakness with a heliotrope rash and Gottron papules, not post-viral calf tenderness.
    - Kawasaki disease needs 5 or more days of fever plus mucocutaneous criteria.
    - Parvovirus B19 affects the joints (symmetric small joints of the hands, wrists and feet), not the muscles.
    - Rheumatic fever follows group A strep and gives carditis, arthritis, nodules, erythema marginatum or chorea, not myalgia.
- **NBME framing lesson:** the stem never says influenza. It describes a flu-like illness that resolved on ibuprofen. The key names both the trigger and the tissue, while the decoys all fish on the pattern of a fever followed by a new complaint.
- **Conflict:** none. Do not carry the explanation's misspelling of creatine kinase.

## Q11 Vulvovaginal candidiasis in a 5-year-old (peds-cms-dd1427)

- **Sort:** new angle. There is also a minor conflict in a different brief, below.
- **Owning brief:** diaper-dermatitis, which already teaches Candida treated with topical nystatin.
- **Why this is not covered:** diaper-dermatitis covers infants in diapers only. newborn-hormone lists candidal vaginitis only as a differential row for a newborn. No item has a prepubertal, toilet-trained girl with vulvovaginitis, and none has STI or HSV drugs as the look-alikes.
- **What must be taught:**
  - **Deciding finding:** a 5-year-old girl with dysuria and redness over the vulva and whole perineum, reaching the perianal skin, with white discharge at the introitus. There are no vesicles or pustules, she is afebrile, and abuse is denied. This is candidal vulvovaginitis.
  - **Key:** topical nystatin. The NBME explanation says superficial candidiasis is treated with a topical antifungal, and that topical agents are preferred over systemic ones for superficial infection.
  - **Traplines:**
    - IM ceftriaxone with oral azithromycin treats gonorrhea and chlamydia, which are sexually transmitted and give mucopurulent discharge. The page adds that an STI in a prepubertal child outside the newborn period is an abuse evaluation (newborn-hormone pearl).
    - Oral acyclovir is for HSV, which gives painful vesicles; there are none here.
    - Oral amoxicillin fits the cystitis pull from the dysuria, but the external rash and white discharge point to fungal vulvovaginitis.
    - Oral metronidazole: see the caution below.
- **NBME framing lesson:** the stem opens with burning on urination to pull toward a UTI and closes on a pending culture. The answer is in the white discharge plus confluent perineal-to-perianal redness.
- **Caution:** the NBME rationale for metronidazole calls it a treatment for systemic fungal infection. That is not accurate, so do not transfer it. A metronidazole trapline needs a guideline-sourced reason.
- **Conflict (minor, in newborn-hormone):** the page says candidal vaginitis in a prepubertal child "essentially requires" recent antibiotics or immunosuppression (differential row and Class-vs-member trapline). The NBME keys candidiasis in a 5-year-old who takes no medications and has no serious illness.
  - **Resolution:** NBME sets the key and framing, and current guidelines set the fact. Check the claim against a current source and soften it to "uncommon without a risk factor" rather than a requirement.
  - **Keep the risk-factor teaching:** the NBME explanation also lists diabetes, antibiotics and corticosteroids as risk factors.

## Q12 NF1 in an asymptomatic teen: which tumor risk (peds-cms-d74882)

- **Sort:** new angle.
- **Owning brief:** tumor-syndromes. sellar-mass is related because it owns optic pathway glioma as a mass.
- **Why this is not covered:** q_f48046d17f7b58fcb6de gives the optic glioma in the stem and asks for NF1. The sellar-mass item gives a symptomatic child with NF1 and asks for MRI. No item goes the other way: from skin findings alone in a well adolescent to the tumor whose risk is raised, with other brain lesions as decoys.
- **What must be taught:**
  - **Deciding finding:** about 10 light brown macules plus axillary freckling in an asymptomatic 17-year-old is NF1. This is the page's café-au-lait gate: six or more, plus freckling.
  - **Key:** optic nerve glioma, usually a low-grade pilocytic astrocytoma of one or both optic nerves or the chiasm. It causes insidious vision loss, proptosis and optic atrophy. Children with NF1 need at least a yearly eye exam. Pheochromocytoma risk is also raised.
  - **Traplines:**
    - Medulloblastoma is the commonest malignant pediatric brain tumor (posterior fossa, raised intracranial pressure, ataxia) but is not linked to NF1.
    - Craniopharyngioma arises from Rathke pouch and gives bitemporal field loss; it is not linked to NF1.
    - Arteriovenous malformation belongs to Wyburn-Mason syndrome, not NF1.
    - Cerebral aneurysm is an adult cause of hemorrhage and is not linked to NF1.
    - bs-posterior-fossa and sellar-mass already own the first two lesions, so the traplines can cross-link.
- **NBME framing lesson:** the syndrome is never named. A sports physical in a well teenager gives the lesions by count, size and site, and the lead-in asks for a risk compared with the general population. That makes it a two-step question: name NF1, then its tumor, with other childhood brain lesions as decoys.
- **Conflict:** none.
- **Gap:** the page's gate uses count and freckling but gives no size threshold. The stem gives sizes (under 1 cm to 2 cm). Adding a size criterion needs a guideline source, because the NBME explanation does not state one.

## Q13 Angelman syndrome: the genetic defect (peds-cms-123615; answered correctly)

- **Sort:** new. The page never mentions Angelman or UBE3A.
- **Nearest content:** Prader-Willi is taught in aneuploidy (key: loss of paternal chromosome 15 genes) and in aq-infant-hypotonia (DNA methylation of 15q11-q13). Rett is in cerebral-palsy.
- **Proposed brief:** id bs-angelman, system nervous system with genetics, data-bp="neuro gen".
  - **Pairs with:** aneuploidy and aq-infant-hypotonia (the Prader-Willi mirror), cerebral-palsy (Rett), tumor-syndromes (the 11p15.5 imprinting item).
  - **Lighter alternative:** backfill an Angelman mirror row and one item in aneuploidy, if a new brief is not wanted for a correct answer.
- **What must be taught:**
  - **Deciding finding:** a 3-year-old girl with seizures, microcephaly, no words, a stiff-legged ataxic gait, hand flapping, and a happy, excitable affect with frequent laughter. She also has a flat occiput, a prominent jaw, widely spaced teeth and strabismus. This is Angelman syndrome.
  - **Key:** deletion of the maternally inherited 15q11-13 region, which holds UBE3A. In the affected brain regions the paternal copy is silenced by imprinting, so losing the maternal copy leaves no working gene.
  - **Traplines:**
    - A paternally inherited chromosome 15 deletion is Prader-Willi (hyperphagia, hypogonadism, short stature). This is a name-swap decoy: same chromosome, parent flipped.
    - An MECP2 mutation is Rett syndrome: a girl who regresses, with hand-wringing rather than flapping.
    - A 7q11.23 deletion is Williams syndrome: friendly demeanor, elfin facies, elastin vessel and valve disease.
    - Trisomy 15 is lethal in utero as a full trisomy and appears only as a uniparental disomy route to Angelman.
    - Trisomy 21 is Down syndrome.
- **NBME framing lesson:** the key and its strongest decoy differ by one word, maternal versus paternal. The behavioral phenotype (laughter, hand flapping, ataxia) decides which parent's copy is missing.
- **Conflict:** none. The page's note that methylation testing detects deletion, uniparental disomy and imprinting defects agrees with the NBME's disomy route.

## Q14 Perinatal HIV: recurrent infections with high immunoglobulins (peds-cms-ae7cd7)

- **Sort:** conflict.
- **Owning brief:** ig-panel.
- **Items with the same key:** q_97a72b96076854f5bbf0 (a 4-month-old with thrush, Pneumocystis, generalized adenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly, keyed perinatal HIV) and q_f3c2c129a5005c9aa121 (a 6-month-old with recurrent thrush, otitis and a CD4 count of 180). They share the key and the thrush, adenopathy and organomegaly findings. The NBME adds two new deciding findings: an AIDS-suggestive maternal history and an immunoglobulin panel with every isotype elevated.
- **What conflicts:** the page says the reverse about IgM in HIV in two places.
  - The ig-panel differential row for HIV says IgG and IgA may be elevated "while IgM is low".
  - The HIV trapline says chronic HIV raises IgG and IgA and lowers IgM, "the mirror image" of the hyper-IgM panel.
  - The NBME panel keyed as HIV has IgA, IgG and IgM all above normal, with IgM about twice the upper limit.
- **Fix:** NBME sets the key and framing; current guidelines set the fact. Verify the IgM claim against a current source, and if the NBME is confirmed, change it to polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia in which IgM can be high as well. Then rest the hyper-IgM versus HIV trapline on the discriminators the page already has: hyper-IgM has a low IgG and a normal CD4:CD8 ratio, while HIV has a high IgG, leukopenia and an inverted ratio.
- **What must be taught (after the fix):**
  - **Deciding finding:** a 2-year-old with recurrent otitis, bacterial pneumonias, pneumococcal bacteremia and persistent thrush. She has adenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, weight at the 5th percentile and thrombocytopenia. Her mother has persistent oral candidiasis, chronic diarrhea and weight loss. This is perinatally acquired HIV.
  - **Key:** HIV infection. With every isotype high, the primary antibody deficiencies (SCID, CVID) are excluded, because both have low immunoglobulins. Per the NBME explanation, confirm with a viral load and start antiretroviral therapy once HIV is confirmed.
  - **Traplines:**
    - SCID presents in infancy with lymphopenia, low immunoglobulins and no palpable nodes. The page already says adenopathy with hepatosplenomegaly separates HIV from SCID.
    - Wiskott-Aldrich: the low platelets are its lure, but it needs eczema and small platelets, and it is X-linked while this patient is a girl.
    - Chronic granulomatous disease gives catalase-positive infections with normal immunoglobulins.
    - Chédiak-Higashi gives albinism and neuropathy.
    - Terminal complement deficiency gives Neisseria infections.
    - Cystic fibrosis gives lung and pancreatic disease, with no thrush or adenopathy.
- **NBME framing lesson:** the lead-in asks for a mechanism of disease but the options are disease names. The clue that decides it is in the mother's history, not the child's. A panel with every isotype high reverses the usual reflex to look for a primary immunodeficiency.
- **Transfer caution:** in the NBME explanation, the choice letters do not match the option list (for example, Chédiak-Higashi is labeled with the letter of the cystic fibrosis option). The explanation also says cervical adenopathy, while the stem gives axillary nodes. Transfer options by their text, not by letter, and take the node site from the stem.
